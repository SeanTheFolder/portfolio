Essay · AI Governance & Data Boundaries

AI Governance Is Data Governance With Extra Steps

Most AI governance debates skip the unglamorous part: deciding what data the model can see, what prompts it can take, and where its outputs can land. Build the boundary first. The agent is the interface. The boundary is the governance.

For Security & Data Officers ~12 min read Published May 2026 Field essay, security & AI practice

A great deal of the AI governance conversation in 2026 is happening one layer too high. It is happening at the level of policies that bless or forbid specific tools, ethics committees that issue position papers, vendor questionnaires that ask whether the model is hallucinating less than it used to. All of those have a place. None of them is the governance. The governance is at the layer below: which data the model can read, which prompts it can accept, which outputs it can produce, and where those outputs are allowed to land. If the boundary at that layer is not set in the same identity, classification, and policy plane that already governs the rest of the data, the policy upstairs is not enforceable, and the ethics committee is writing position papers about a system whose actual behavior nobody is bounding.

This essay is the operational version of that argument. It is written for the security officer or data governance lead who is being asked to "stand up AI governance" by a leadership team that has read about AI governance in the trade press and would like there to be some. The most useful answer is that AI governance is data governance with extra steps, and the extra steps are smaller than they sound when the data governance is already in place.

The premise: the agent is the interface, the boundary is the governance

An AI agent — Copilot, a custom Copilot Studio agent, a vendor's clinical assistant, a homegrown LangChain pipeline — is a piece of software that reads from a corpus, accepts a prompt from a user, and produces an output. Every word in that sentence already has a governance regime that predates the agent. "Reads from a corpus" is a data access decision governed by identity and classification. "Accepts a prompt from a user" is an authentication and authorization decision. "Produces an output" is a DLP decision. "Where the output lands" is a labeling and storage decision.

The mistake most organizations make is to treat the agent as a new kind of system that requires a new kind of governance. The reality is that the agent operates against the same identity plane, the same classification plane, the same DLP plane, and the same audit plane as the underlying tenant. If those planes are configured correctly, the agent inherits the right behavior. If they are not, no amount of agent-specific policy compensates for the gaps.

The leverage point is the data boundary. Build it once, in the place every other tool already respects, and the agent layer becomes a question of which capabilities to expose and to whom, not a question of how to invent a parallel governance regime.

The four data boundaries that matter

Every AI agent decision reduces to one of four boundaries. Each one already has a control plane in a well-run M365 tenant.

Boundary one: what the model can see. The corpus the agent retrieves from is the largest single determinant of risk. A Copilot deployment that grounds against the entire SharePoint footprint will surface anything that footprint exposes. The boundary is set by sensitivity labels on the source content, by the underlying SharePoint and OneDrive permissions, and by Purview's data classification policies. The agent does not need a new permission model; it needs to be subject to the existing one.

The most common audit finding here is over-permissioned shared drives that were tolerable when the only readers were humans clicking through folders, and that became a real risk when an agent could synthesize the contents of a thousand folders into a single answer. The right time to discover this is during an access review against the corpus, before the agent goes live, not in the agent's first week of production use.

Boundary two: what the model can be prompted with. The prompt is data flowing in. Users will paste anything into a prompt — patient names, internal financials, draft contracts, screenshots of EHR pages. If prompts route to a model the organization does not control, those pastes are exfiltration. Conditional Access policies for AI applications, sensitivity-label-aware DLP on copy and paste, and tenant-attached models (Copilot rather than consumer ChatGPT) collapse most of this risk by design.

The most common configuration error here is permitting users to access consumer AI tools alongside the sanctioned enterprise tools, on the theory that "blocking is hard." Blocking is not hard if the sanctioned tool is good enough. The sanctioned tool being good enough is a product decision the IT and security team can make in advance.

Boundary three: what outputs the model can produce. The output is data flowing out. A model that has seen sensitive source content can summarize it, paraphrase it, and translate it across formats in ways that bypass naive DLP rules looking for the original strings. Sensitivity-label inheritance — where the output inherits the most-restrictive label from any of its source documents — is the control that closes this gap. Without it, label-based DLP becomes a porous filter that rewards the model for laundering classified content into derivative form.

Boundary four: where outputs are allowed to land. A summary saved to a Word file inside the same tenant inherits the controls of the tenant. A summary copied to a personal Gmail draft does not. The control plane here is a combination of endpoint DLP, browser extension policy, and Conditional Access rules that govern which apps the user can sign into and which downloads are permitted from sanctioned AI tools. None of this is new. All of it is the same plane that governs ordinary data movement.

1
The Boundary-First Rule

Build the four data boundaries in Purview, Conditional Access, and DLP before the first agent goes live. The agent is the interface; the boundaries are the governance. An agent stood up against unlabeled, over-permissioned source data is a governance gap with a chat box on top.

The order of operations matters

Most AI governance failures I have seen come from getting the order of operations wrong. The wrong order is: pick an agent, deploy the agent, write a usage policy, then go back and try to install the data boundaries. The right order is: install the data boundaries, validate them with a small AI deployment, then expand the agent surface against an environment that is already governed.

This is not pedantry about sequencing. It is about what happens during the deployment phase. If the boundaries do not exist when the agent ships, the agent will surface ungoverned content for some period, and the organization will accumulate exposure in production while the boundary work is happening in parallel. If the boundaries exist first, the agent's first day in production is its safest day, not its riskiest. The team that runs the agent gets to operate against a known-good corpus instead of fighting a series of small incidents while the labels and DLP rules catch up.

The other reason the order matters is political. A leadership team that approves an AI deployment after seeing the boundaries already in place treats the deployment as a bounded addition to existing infrastructure. A leadership team that approves an AI deployment first and is then asked to invest in data governance treats the data governance as an additional cost to a project they thought was finished. The same dollar amount lands very differently depending on the framing.

What goes wrong when you start with the agent

The standard failure pattern looks like this. A pilot Copilot deployment goes live for an executive subset. The executives are pleased; the productivity gains are real. Word spreads. The tenant-wide rollout is approved before anyone has audited the underlying SharePoint structure. Within a month, the first incident lands: a user has prompted Copilot for "the most recent merger discussions" and the model has surfaced a deck from a folder that was over-permissioned three years ago. The deck was not classified. The folder access was a known-old issue that nobody had cleaned up because there was no acute pressure to. The agent produced the acute pressure on day twenty-three of production use, in front of a reader who should not have seen the content.

The fix at that point is reactive: pull the over-permissioned folders, classify the content, train the access review process, audit the prompt logs for similar exposures. The organization will spend three months doing in production what should have been six weeks of pre-deployment work. The governance program will be defined by the incident rather than by intention. And the leadership team that approved the rollout will be asked to fund the cleanup as an unexpected cost, which is the political position that most damages the security program's credibility.

None of this is hypothetical. Variants of it are happening across mid-market healthcare in 2026, because the agent layer is exciting and the data layer is not, and the order of operations is set by enthusiasm rather than risk.

Sensitivity labels do most of the work

If a single control plane has to be picked as the most important for AI governance, it is sensitivity labeling. Labels carry through to the agent's grounding, to the agent's output, to the DLP rules that govern where the output can land, and to the audit log that proves what happened. A tenant with a working sensitivity label taxonomy and consistent application is most of the way to AI governance. A tenant without one is most of the way to an incident.

The honest answer is that label adoption is a multi-month project, not a configuration change. It involves building the taxonomy (most organizations land on something like Public, Internal, Confidential, Highly Confidential, Restricted), defining the auto-labeling rules (file content, location, sensitive-info types), defining the user-facing labels (the dropdown in Office), and managing the migration of existing content to labeled state. The work is real. So is the value. The label is the unit of governance once the agent layer is in play.

2
The Inherited Label Rule

An AI output inherits the most restrictive label of its source content. If the model summarizes one Confidential document and ten Public ones, the summary is Confidential. Any DLP regime that treats the output as Public because it does not contain the literal source strings is broken by design.

Conditional Access as the AI app gate

Identity is the second control plane. Conditional Access policies that scope AI application access by user, role, device state, network location, and risk score are how an organization decides who can talk to which model under which circumstances. The same policy framework that governs whether a contractor can access the EHR governs whether a contractor can talk to the internal Copilot. Treat the AI applications as first-class apps in the Conditional Access policy set, not as a separate regime.

The specific decisions that matter most: require compliant or hybrid-joined devices for any AI app that can reach internal data; require phishing-resistant MFA for any AI app with administrative capability against the data corpus; block consumer AI tools at the network or endpoint layer unless the organization has consciously decided to allow them with caveats; and route AI app sign-ins to the same risk-based session controls that govern other sensitive applications.

None of this is new control engineering. It is the application of an existing identity discipline to a new class of application. The reason it has to be deliberate is that the AI tooling vendors will offer convenience-oriented integrations that route around Conditional Access if not specifically configured to honor it. The vendor is optimizing for sign-up velocity. The organization is optimizing for governance posture. The organization has to choose.

The audit trail problem nobody plans for

The fourth control plane is audit. AI deployments produce logs, but the logs are often shaped by the vendor's product priorities rather than by the organization's audit requirements. Prompts and responses, the source documents grounded against, the user identity, the device state, and the timestamp need to be retained in a queryable form for the period the organization's data retention policy specifies. Most default configurations either retain less than this or retain it in a vendor-specific format that does not integrate with the SIEM the rest of the security program relies on.

The work to fix this is to define the audit schema before the deployment, configure the agent or the agent's underlying platform to emit it, route the logs to the central platform (Microsoft Sentinel for Microsoft-stack tenants, the vendor's analogue otherwise), and build the dashboards and detections that turn the log stream into operational signal. Without this, the AI deployment is a black box that the security program cannot meaningfully investigate when something goes wrong.

A policy without an audit trail is an aspiration. An audit trail without a policy is an inventory of incidents. Both are required.

Why this turns AI governance into a compliance asset

The unexpected benefit of building AI governance against the existing data governance plane is that the AI program becomes a compliance asset rather than a compliance liability. A regulator or board member asking "how do you govern AI" gets the same answer as "how do you govern data": through identity, classification, DLP, and audit. The AI deployment is a new application against a known control plane. The boundary is set in places the organization can show, on policies the organization already maintains, with audit trails the organization already retains.

Compare this to the alternative answer, where AI governance is a separate program managed by a separate committee with separate policies and a separate vendor stack. That answer is harder to explain, harder to maintain, and harder to defend during an audit. It also accumulates duplicate cost, because every additional governance plane is something the organization has to fund, staff, and keep current as the underlying technology shifts.

The lesson is not that AI is unimportant. It is that the controls that make AI safe are the same controls that make the rest of the data estate safe. Investing in data governance returns dividends across every system that touches data, of which AI is one important and visible class. Investing in AI-specific governance returns dividends only against AI, and only until the next class of system shows up and needs its own governance regime.

What a working AI policy actually looks like

A defensible AI governance policy is shorter than most committees produce. It says, in plain language: the sanctioned AI tools, the data the tools may access (by sensitivity label), the prompts that are out of scope (PHI to consumer tools, anything Restricted, anything subject to legal hold), the outputs that require human review before use, the audit retention period, and the named owner of the policy. It points at the underlying technical controls (Conditional Access policies, DLP rules, label taxonomy) for the enforcement layer. It is signed by the security officer, the privacy officer, and the data governance lead, and it is reviewed quarterly.

What it does not do is enumerate every possible use case, take a position on every ethical question, or commit to controls that are not actually wired up. Policies that do those things are aspirational. The aspirational policy is downstream of the program; it is not the program.
