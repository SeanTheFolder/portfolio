Field Notes · Vendor Risk Management

The BAA Is Not the Control

A practical vendor risk program for healthcare. The BAA is the legal instrument. The control is what you actually do to inspect, segment, and decommission third-party access. A field guide for the small team that has to make this work without a procurement department of its own.

For Security & Operations Leaders ~12 min read Published May 2026 Field essay, healthcare practice

A friend who runs a small healthcare nonprofit once described their vendor risk program in a single sentence: "We get the BAA signed and then we move on." It was an honest description, and it is also a description of the most common failure pattern in healthcare vendor risk. The signed Business Associate Agreement creates the legal relationship. It does not create a control. The control is the set of operational disciplines that make the relationship safe in practice: how the vendor's access is provisioned, what they can see, how their work is monitored, and what happens when the engagement ends. Most healthcare organizations have the legal instrument and not the control, and most of the third-party breaches in healthcare in 2025 and 2026 happened in the gap between the two.

This essay is the operational version of vendor risk for an organization that does not have an enterprise procurement function, a dedicated TPRM analyst, or the time to deploy a six-figure vendor risk platform. Most home health, hospice, and mid-market healthcare organizations are in this category. The framing is what to actually do, in what order, with the team you have, and what to stop pretending matters.

The premise: the legal instrument is downstream of the operational control

When a healthcare organization signs a BAA with a vendor, the document does several things. It establishes that the vendor is acting as a Business Associate under HIPAA. It commits the vendor to use and disclose PHI only as permitted by the agreement. It establishes the vendor's breach notification obligations to the covered entity. It allocates indemnification, audit rights, and termination terms. All of this is necessary. None of it is the control.

The control is what the organization does between the signed BAA and the vendor's eventual offboarding. The vendor will, in practice, be granted some subset of the organization's data, identity surface, and network reach. The size of that subset, the way it is monitored, and the speed with which it can be revoked are the actual security posture of the engagement. A signed BAA against an over-permissioned vendor is a legally clean breach.

Reframe: the BAA is an instrument that allocates liability if the operational controls fail. The operational controls are what make the failure unlikely.

The vendor inventory is the foundation

The first artifact a vendor risk program requires is the vendor inventory. Not the procurement system's vendor list, which is typically a record of who the organization paid. The inventory of every vendor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on the organization's behalf, plus every vendor whose product touches the organization's identity, network, or endpoint estate even if it does not handle PHI. The two sets overlap but are not identical, and both are in scope for vendor risk.

The inventory has a small number of fields per vendor: the legal entity, the product or service, the categories of data the vendor can access, the systems they integrate with, the type of access (federated SSO, service account, API key, on-site network appliance, physical access), the named business owner inside the organization, the BAA status, the cyber insurance status, and the renewal or contract end date. Six fields, maybe eight if compliance attestations matter for the regulator at hand. The discipline is keeping it current.

Most organizations discover, on first inventory pass, that they have between fifty and three hundred vendors that meet one or both criteria. The number is almost always larger than leadership expects. The largest categories are usually clinical SaaS (the EHR, the e-prescribing layer, the lab interface vendors), administrative SaaS (HR, payroll, billing), security tooling (the SIEM, the EDR, the email security vendor), and the long tail of departmental tools that grew up in shadow IT. Each one is a third-party touch point, and each one needs a position in the inventory.

1
The Inventory Rule

An organization without a current vendor inventory does not have a vendor risk program. It has a folder of BAAs. The inventory is the artifact that makes risk decisions possible; the BAAs are the legal scaffolding around the decisions.

Tiering: not every vendor warrants the same scrutiny

The mistake that kills most vendor risk programs in their first year is treating every vendor at the same level of due diligence. The team gets buried in the long tail, runs out of capacity, and the program loses credibility. The fix is honest tiering.

A practical tier scheme has three or four levels. Tier 1 vendors are the ones whose failure or compromise would be a reportable HIPAA breach for the organization, would halt clinical operations, or would expose the largest financial counterparties. The EHR. The e-prescribing layer. The MSSP, if there is one. Tier 1 vendors get the full treatment: annual security assessment, SOC 2 Type II review, incident notification SLA, named vendor relationship owner, quarterly check-in. Most organizations have between five and fifteen Tier 1 vendors.

Tier 2 vendors are meaningful but not catastrophic. The HR information system. The patient communication tool. The accounts-payable platform. They get a lighter assessment cadence (every two years), a SOC 2 review at onboarding, and a formal review at contract renewal. Most organizations have twenty to fifty Tier 2 vendors.

Tier 3 vendors are the long tail. The departmental scheduling tool. The conference-room booking app. The wellness portal. They get a one-time security review at onboarding and an annual confirmation that they still exist and still have a current BAA where required. Most organizations have one to two hundred Tier 3 vendors.

The honest tiering is the part most organizations skip. They want to apply Tier 1 rigor to every vendor on principle, fail to do so in practice, and end up with a program that is theoretically rigorous and operationally brittle. A program that does Tier 1 rigor on the fifteen Tier 1 vendors, Tier 2 review on the right thirty, and stops pretending the long tail will get questionnaire-level treatment is a program that actually runs.

The questionnaire problem

Most vendor risk programs are organized around questionnaires. A vendor sends back a 200-question security attestation, the security team reads it, files it, and the engagement proceeds. The questionnaire is a control theater for two reasons. First, the answers are self-attested and rarely audited; vendors learn to write the answers that pass review. Second, the answers describe the vendor's general security program, not the specific configuration of the access the vendor has to the organization. A vendor that scores well on a SIG-Lite questionnaire can still be over-permissioned in the buying organization's tenant.

The fix is not a longer questionnaire. The fix is to spend the questionnaire budget on three high-leverage artifacts and stop pretending the rest matters.

Artifact one: the SOC 2 Type II report. A current SOC 2 Type II report from a credentialed auditor is the closest thing to a real third-party security attestation that the market produces. Read the bridge letter. Read the exceptions. Read the auditor's name. A vendor that cannot produce one for a Tier 1 or Tier 2 engagement is a vendor that has not invested in independent verification of its controls, and that fact is itself diagnostic.

Artifact two: the architecture diagram with the access boundary. A one-page diagram showing where the vendor's access lands in the organization's environment, what permissions it carries, and what data it touches. The vendor often cannot produce this; the buying organization usually can, and producing it as part of onboarding is more useful than reading any questionnaire. The diagram is also the artifact that survives the personnel turnover on both sides.

Artifact three: the breach notification SLA in writing. The BAA will reference breach notification. The operational artifact is the named contact, the channel, the time-to-notification commitment, and the test of the channel. A vendor whose breach notification process exists only in the BAA has a notification process that exists only on paper.

A vendor that scores well on a 200-question SIG response can still be over-permissioned in your tenant. The questionnaire is a control theater. The configuration is the control.

Access boundaries are the actual lever

The single highest-leverage move in vendor risk management is the operational discipline around the vendor's access to the organization's environment. Not the contract. Not the questionnaire. The access.

The boundaries that matter:

Identity. Every vendor user accessing the organization's systems should authenticate through the organization's identity provider, with MFA, against a Conditional Access policy that scopes their access by role, device state, and network. Federated SSO into Entra ID with named accounts, not shared logins. Service accounts, where required, should be inventoried, rotated, and monitored. The number of vendor users with administrative privileges should be a board-reportable metric.

Network. Vendor traffic into the organization's environment should be segmented from internal traffic. Site-to-site VPNs to vendor environments should be terminated at a controlled boundary, not poured into the production VLAN. Vendor access to clinical systems should be visible to the SIEM and detectable by the same rules that govern internal access.

Data. The vendor's read access to PHI should be scoped to the data they need, not the data the system happens to expose. The most common violation here is a vendor with API access that returns more than it asked for; the fix is at the API layer, not the BAA. Sensitivity labels and DLP rules should apply to data flowing to vendor environments the same way they apply to data flowing internally.

Endpoint. Vendor-supplied appliances on the organization's network are a population unto themselves. They run an embedded operating system that the vendor patches on the vendor's schedule. They authenticate to vendor cloud services that the buying organization has limited visibility into. They are a real piece of the attack surface and they almost never appear on the asset management dashboard. The fix is to inventory them, segment them, and monitor them with the same discipline as any other endpoint class.

The decommissioning problem

Most vendor risk programs spend ninety percent of their attention on onboarding and ten percent on decommissioning. The ratio should be closer to fifty-fifty. The decommissioning is harder, less visible, and more frequently missed.

What decommissioning actually requires:

  • Revoke vendor user accounts on the day the contract ends, not on the day someone remembers
  • Disable service accounts and API keys; rotate any shared secrets
  • Remove vendor IPs from any allow-lists
  • Confirm the vendor has returned or destroyed the organization's PHI per the BAA termination clause; obtain written attestation
  • Recover any vendor-supplied equipment
  • Update the vendor inventory to inactive status with a date stamp
  • Cancel the recurring billing if procurement has not already

Six or seven discrete steps. Three or four of them get done at most organizations; the others slip. The most common slip is service accounts and API keys, which often live for years past the engagement they were created for. The second most common is the written attestation of data destruction, which the vendor will provide on request and almost no one requests.

2
The Decommissioning Rule

An engagement that ends without a documented decommissioning is an engagement that has not actually ended. The vendor's access persists; their attestation of data destruction does not exist; the organization's exposure continues for as long as the gap. Onboarding without a paired decommissioning playbook is half a program.

The annual review is where the program gets real

An annual cycle ties the program together. Each Tier 1 vendor gets a thirty-minute review. Each Tier 2 vendor gets a fifteen-minute review on a two-year cadence. The agenda is the same every time: confirm the inventory record is current, confirm the BAA and SOC 2 are current, walk the access boundary against the diagram, review any incidents in the last period, walk the renewal date and the contract terms, and decide whether the engagement continues, renegotiates, or terminates.

The output of the annual review is a one-line decision per vendor: continue at current scope, expand, contract, or terminate. The decisions roll up into the quarterly board risk dashboard as a count of vendors at each posture, plus the Tier 1 vendor list with named status. A board that sees this view once a quarter has a real understanding of third-party risk; a board that does not see it has a vague sense that the program exists.

The team you have, not the team you wish you had

The hardest part of running a vendor risk program at a small healthcare organization is that the work is real and the team is small. A working program at a 500-person organization typically takes about a quarter to a third of an FTE distributed across the security officer, a privacy lead, and IT operations. It cannot be entirely on the security officer; it cannot be a side project for a single analyst; it cannot survive without a named owner per Tier 1 vendor.

The right operational shape is a security officer who owns the framework, an IT operations lead who owns the access plumbing, and a procurement-adjacent person who owns the contract metadata. The three of them meet for an hour a quarter to review the vendor inventory and the decision queue. Outside of that hour, each of them runs their slice of the program inside their normal day. Healthcare organizations that try to centralize the entire program with one person tend to lose the program to that person's other responsibilities.

Why this matters more in 2026 than it did in 2024

The rate of third-party breaches affecting healthcare has continued to climb through 2025 and into 2026. The largest breaches reported by HHS in the last twelve months were not direct compromises of covered entities; they were compromises of vendors with access to multiple covered entities at once. The economics of attacking a single SaaS vendor with twenty hospital customers are better than attacking the hospitals one at a time, and attackers have noticed.

The implication for a buying organization is that vendor risk is no longer a compliance overlay on top of the security program. It is a structural part of the security program, and the budget should reflect that. An organization that spends ninety percent of its security budget on internal controls and ten percent on vendor risk is solving a smaller subset of the actual problem than its threat model implies. A more honest split is closer to seventy-thirty.

The other implication is that the regulator's posture is shifting. OCR has signaled, and litigation has confirmed, that a covered entity that delegated PHI to an inadequately controlled vendor is on the hook for the consequences regardless of the BAA's indemnification language. The legal instrument allocates liability after the fact. It does not eliminate it.
