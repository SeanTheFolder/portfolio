Reading a Cyber Insurance Renewal Like a CFO

The renewal application is the most underused security artifact in the building. Read it as a structured external assessment of your control set, map the questions to CIS v8, and turn premium negotiation into a board-ready posture conversation. Twelve months of program work, scoped by the carrier whether you cooperate or not.

For Boards, CFOs & CISOs · ~12 min read · Published May 2026 · Field essay, healthcare practice

The renewal binder lands in the CFO's inbox in late summer. The questionnaire runs forty pages. The first time I saw one, I made the same mistake most security teams make: I treated it as a procurement chore, filled it out as quickly as I could, and watched the premium go up anyway. The second time, I read it as the most structured external assessment of the security program the organization had ever received. The premium did not just stop going up; the answers became the agenda for the next twelve months of program work, the artifact for the board, and the lever that produced a measurable reduction at renewal. Cyber insurance applications are the most underused security document in the building. This essay is the version of how to read them that I wish someone had handed me four renewals ago.

The framing is for the CFO who signs the check, the CISO who answers the questions, and the board member who has to vote on the program. All three need a shared language for what the renewal is actually telling the organization, and the renewal itself is a remarkably good source of that language if you read it the right way.

The premise: the carrier is doing your control assessment for free

The cyber insurance market in 2026 is not the market it was in 2021. Carriers have lost meaningful money on healthcare cyber claims, particularly ransomware and business interruption, and they have responded by tightening underwriting. The application questionnaire is the instrument. The questions are not arbitrary; they are calibrated to the controls that correlate with claim outcomes in the carrier's own actuarial data. Multi-factor authentication on remote access. Privileged access management for administrative accounts. Backups with off-network or immutable copies. Email security with link rewriting and impersonation protection. Endpoint detection and response, deployed and tuned. Patch management with measurable cadence. Incident response with tabletop frequency. Each of those questions is on the form because the carrier has paid claims to organizations that did not have it.

What that means for the buying organization is that the questionnaire is an external, opinionated, evidence-anchored assessment of the security program against controls the market has decided are load-bearing. The organization is paying for it whether it reads it that way or not. The decision is whether to extract the value or merely fill out the form.

Read carefully, the questionnaire reveals three things. First, what the carrier believes the program should look like. Second, where the organization actually is against that belief. Third, what the gap is worth in dollar premium terms. All three matter for the board conversation that follows.

The mapping: questionnaire to CIS v8

Most healthcare-specific cyber insurance questionnaires map cleanly to a subset of the CIS Critical Security Controls v8. Not all of v8; the parts that move claim outcomes. Roughly:

  • Identity and access (CIS Controls 5, 6) — MFA on every external interface, MFA on every privileged role, conditional access, privileged access management, named account discipline. The single largest cluster of questions and the single largest premium driver.
  • Backup and recovery (CIS Control 11) — backup completeness, off-network or immutable copies, restore testing cadence, ransomware recovery time objective. The second-largest cluster, and the one that determines whether the policy will respond to a ransomware claim at all.
  • Email security (CIS Control 9) — DMARC, link rewriting, attachment sandboxing, phishing simulation, user training. Smaller premium driver than backups, but a required floor.
  • Endpoint security (CIS Control 10) — EDR deployment, coverage rate, response capability, isolated-from-internet servers covered or explained. Increasingly central to underwriting.
  • Vulnerability management (CIS Control 7) — patching cadence, internet-facing system inventory, time-to-patch SLAs, scanning frequency.
  • Incident response (CIS Control 17) — written IR plan, tested in the last twelve months, third-party retainer for digital forensics.
  • Logging and detection (CIS Control 8) — centralized logging, SIEM coverage, alert response process.
  • Vendor and third-party risk (CIS Control 15) — BAA management, vendor security review, segmentation of vendor access.

The mapping is not exact, but it is close enough that an organization that builds a clean CIS v8 program is, by construction, building the answer to most of the renewal questionnaire. The reverse is also true: an organization that fills out the questionnaire honestly has, by construction, identified the next quarter of CIS v8 program work.

1
The Crosswalk Rule

Maintain one current crosswalk from your CIS v8 implementation status to the renewal questionnaire. The crosswalk is the artifact that lets the security team produce renewal answers in a week instead of a month, and lets the CFO understand that the program work and the premium work are the same work.

How to actually answer the questionnaire

The mistake most teams make is to answer the questionnaire on optimism. The MFA question gets a yes because MFA is configured even though it is not enforced for all users. The backup question gets a yes because backups exist even though immutable copies do not. The phishing simulation question gets a yes because there was one simulation last year. None of these answers are technically false. All of them will be tested in the event of a claim, and any gap between the answer and the reality is a coverage problem at exactly the moment the organization needs the policy to respond.

The right discipline is to answer the questionnaire on evidence. For each yes, the team should be able to point to the artifact: the Conditional Access policy export, the backup retention configuration screenshot, the phishing simulation results report, the IR tabletop attendance roster. The answer is what the evidence supports. If the evidence does not support a yes, the answer is no, and the gap becomes a remediation item.

This sounds like extra work. It is. The work is also the same work the security team would do in the first month of an incident response, when the carrier's claims adjuster asks for evidence of the controls the organization claimed at renewal. Doing that work in advance, with calm, before money is on the line, produces a better answer and a better posture. Doing it under claim pressure produces neither.

The four questions that move the premium most

Across the dozens of healthcare cyber insurance applications I have seen, four questions move the premium most. Carriers weight differently, but the pattern is consistent.

Question one: Is MFA enforced on all remote access for all users, including administrators? A clean yes, with evidence, with no exceptions, is the single largest premium reducer. A yes with exceptions is a yellow flag. A no is a hard underwriting line at most carriers in 2026, meaning the policy will either be denied or will carry a ransomware sublimit so low it is functionally unusable.

Question two: Are backups protected against ransomware (immutable, off-network, or air-gapped) and tested in the last ninety days? A no on this question, in 2026, makes the policy nearly worthless against the threat that drives most healthcare cyber claims. Carriers have priced this directly into the structure of the coverage. An organization with strong backups can negotiate; an organization without them cannot.

Question three: Is there an EDR product deployed across the endpoint estate with greater than 95% coverage? The threshold matters. 95% is the typical underwriting floor. Below that, the policy will price up; below 80%, the policy may carve out endpoints not covered. Coverage rate is the metric, not the product brand.

Question four: Is there a written incident response plan, tested in the last twelve months, with a digital forensics retainer in place? The "tested" part is what separates the yes from the no. A plan that exists only on paper is not a plan; the carrier knows this; the questionnaire asks about the test for that reason. The tabletop is the cheapest control on the list and one of the highest-leverage in the underwriting.

A questionnaire answered on optimism is a coverage gap at the worst possible time. The answers will be tested when the claim is filed; do the testing now.

The premium negotiation as a posture conversation

Most premium negotiations are run by procurement against the broker. The security team is asked to "answer the questions" and then leaves the room. This is a missed leverage point.

The version that works is to put the security officer in the negotiation, with the broker, in front of the underwriter. The conversation moves from "fill out the form" to "here is what we have, here is the evidence, here is the remediation roadmap for the gaps, here is the timeline." Underwriters in 2026 are calibrated to recognize that an organization with a clean current state and a credible roadmap presents a different risk profile than an organization with the same current state and no roadmap. The premium should differ, and frequently does.

The artifacts the security officer brings to the conversation:

  • The completed questionnaire with evidence references for each yes
  • The CIS v8 implementation status against the questionnaire crosswalk
  • The roadmap of remediation items for each no, with target completion dates
  • A summary of the program governance: who reviews this with the board, on what cadence
  • If applicable, a copy of the most recent independent assessment (penetration test, SOC 2, HHS audit, third-party readiness review)

An underwriter who reviews this packet can price the program, not just the form. The premium difference between a well-presented program and a poorly-presented one is meaningful. It compounds across years.

The three numbers the board should see at renewal

Boards typically see one number at cyber insurance renewal: the premium. That number is not enough to govern the program. The board should see three.

Number one: the premium and how it changed. Year-over-year change in premium, against the carrier's published rate movement, against industry benchmarks. A premium that fell against an industry that rose is signal. A premium that rose with the market is parity. A premium that rose faster than the market is a control problem that needs explaining.

Number two: the coverage limit and the major sublimits. Total tower limit, ransomware sublimit, business interruption sublimit, regulatory defense sublimit. Sublimits are where coverage is actually carved up; the headline limit can be reassuring while the sublimit that matters most for the organization's threat profile is small. The board should understand the sublimits, not just the tower.

Number three: the renewal questionnaire deltas year-over-year. What did the carrier ask this year that it did not ask last year? What did the organization answer differently than last year, and why? This is the leading indicator of where the threat landscape is moving and where the program needs to invest. A board that sees this delta is governing forward; a board that sees only the premium is governing backward.

2
The Three-Number Rule

Premium, sublimits, questionnaire delta. Boards that see one of the three are governing the bill. Boards that see all three are governing the risk. The renewal cycle is not a procurement event; it is a governance event with a procurement output.

Where the carrier is wrong, and what to do about it

Carriers are not always right. The questionnaire is calibrated to the carrier's loss data, which is heavily skewed toward the threat profile of the carrier's existing book. A small healthcare nonprofit in home health is a different risk profile than the regional hospital systems that dominate most carriers' loss data, and the questionnaire does not always discriminate. Sometimes the questionnaire treats controls as binary that, at the organization's scale, do not need to be binary. Sometimes it asks for a control posture that is overkill for the actual threat surface.

The right response is not to argue with the questionnaire. The right response is to answer it honestly, then provide a written supplement that explains the calibration. "We do not have a 24/7 SOC because the threat surface and after-hours incident pattern do not justify the cost; we have a 24/7 EDR with a managed detection vendor and the documented escalation path and response time fits the actual threat model." Underwriters will read the supplement. Brokers will help frame it. The premium will, more often than not, recognize the calibration. An organization that cannot articulate why it has the program it has is harder to underwrite than an organization that can.

Why this conversation belongs to the CFO

The cyber insurance renewal is a financial transaction with a security artifact at its center. The CFO's signature is on the policy. The board's vote authorizes the spend. The premium and the deductible affect the income statement. The coverage gaps, when they appear, affect the balance sheet and the reserves. Treating cyber insurance as a security-team responsibility because the questionnaire is technical misallocates the conversation.

The frame that works: the security officer produces the answers and the evidence, the CFO leads the conversation with the broker, the board governs the limits and the residual risk. The security officer is the technical authority on the controls. The CFO is the financial authority on the transfer mechanism. The board is the governance authority on the residual risk after the transfer. Each of the three needs the other two to be in the room. The cyber renewal is one of the few annual events where security, finance, and board governance are forced into the same conversation, and the organizations that exploit that fact run materially better programs than the organizations that do not.
