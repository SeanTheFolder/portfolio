The First Ninety Days as a New Healthcare CISO or IT Director

A week-by-week playbook for a new healthcare security or IT executive, the mistakes that wreck the first thirty days, and the questions hiring managers should be asking at week twelve. Written for both sides of the engagement.

For Hiring Managers & First-Time CISOs ~14 min read Published May 2026 Field essay, healthcare leadership

The first ninety days of a new healthcare CISO or IT Director are not, despite the standard advice, a time for action. They are a time for inventory, listening, and writing. The action comes after, and the credibility for the action depends on doing the listening first. The most common pattern of failure in the role is reversed: the new leader arrives with a roadmap from the prior engagement, starts shipping in week three, and discovers in month nine that the roadmap was solving for a different organization.

This essay is the playbook I would actually run on day one of any new engagement, and the questions a hiring manager or board should be using to evaluate a new CISO or IT Director at the end of the period. It is also, by extension, a description of what the candidate should be saying in the interview about how they intend to spend the first ninety days, since the answer to that question is probably the highest-signal predictor of how the engagement will go.

The premise: ninety days is a contract

The first ninety days are the only period in the engagement when the new leader can be openly in learning mode without losing standing. After day ninety, expectations shift permanently, and any rebuild work that was deferred becomes harder to schedule because the window of "I am still figuring this out" has closed.

That window is also a contract with the organization that hired you. The contract has three deliverables, one per thirty-day phase: a structured listening tour and inventory; a written risk assessment with a prioritized roadmap framed in business terms; and visible execution on a small set of high-leverage items that the executive team has agreed to. Anyone who shows up to the day-ninety review with anything less than these three is in early trouble.

Days 1 through 30: listen, inventory, no changes

Resist the urge to ship anything in the first thirty days. The temptation is constant, especially in environments where the security or IT posture is visibly broken. Resist it anyway. The first month is for understanding the actual operating reality, the political landscape, and the prior history of attempted fixes, because each of those will affect what is realistic to build.

The listening tour

Roughly thirty conversations, scheduled in the first three weeks. Cover every executive, every director-level operational leader, every member of the IT and security team, the audit committee chair if there is one, the legal and compliance lead, the CFO and controller, the head of HR, the heads of clinical operations and intake, the IT and security vendors with active contracts, and a deliberate sample of front-line staff. The conversations are not investigative. They are open: what is working, what is broken, what would you change in the first quarter, what should I have asked that I did not.

The conversations produce three things: a map of the organization's actual decision pathways (which often differ from the org chart), a list of the issues that the organization already knows about and has chosen not to fix (which is the most useful intelligence in the building), and a sense of which leaders will be allies on which categories of work.

The inventory

Concurrent with the listening tour. Build a complete inventory of the systems, identities, vendors, contracts, regulatory commitments, and prior initiatives. The inventory is the foundation that everything else is built on, and it is almost always partially missing on day one.

The Day-30 Inventory

By the end of week four, you should have a complete list of: every active vendor and contract end date, every administrative-credentialed user, every system that touches PHI, every regulatory commitment with a deadline in the next 24 months, every open audit finding, every executed BAA, and every prior security or IT initiative whether completed or abandoned. None of this is optional. All of it is harder to assemble than the prior leadership will have admitted.

The inventory is not the deliverable. It is the substrate that makes the deliverables possible. A CISO who has not done the inventory in the first thirty days is operating from received wisdom and will produce a roadmap that solves for the organization that the prior leadership described, not the organization that exists.

What not to do in days 1 through 30

Do not buy a tool. Do not announce a strategy. Do not reorganize the team. Do not declare a quick win. Do not make any visible commitment to the board beyond the scheduled day-90 review. Do not, under any circumstances, replace a prior leader's pet project with your own pet project, because the political cost will outlast the technical benefit. Each of these is a temptation. Each of them is the single most common way that new healthcare security or IT leaders compromise their own first year.

Days 30 through 60: the written risk assessment

The deliverable for the second phase is a written document. Not a deck. A document, between fifteen and thirty pages, structured the way a board would expect to read it, presenting the actual risk position of the organization at the moment of arrival and a prioritized remediation roadmap framed in business language.

The structure I use:

  • Executive summary, one page. The state of the program in three paragraphs. The one item that is genuinely on fire and needs immediate action. The three items that need decisions in the next quarter. The strategic posture for the next twelve months.
  • The inventory summary. Two to three pages. What exists, what is missing, where the surprises were.
  • The risk register. Ten to fifteen ranked items, each translated into operational, regulatory, or reputational language. Calibrated probability and dollar impact ranges per Hubbard, not heat-map colors.
  • The prioritized roadmap. The next twelve months of work, sequenced and resource-loaded. Each item with a one-line business case and a measurable outcome.
  • The asks. What the program needs from the executive team and the board to deliver the roadmap. Capacity, capital, decision authority on specific items, alignment on specific risk acceptances.
  • The unresolved. What I do not yet have enough information to recommend on, with a date for revisiting.

The document goes to the CEO, the executive team, and the board chair as a pre-read for the day-60 leadership session. The session is structured around three questions: do you accept the risk picture as I have framed it, do you accept the prioritization, and do you accept the asks. Disagreement on any of the three is information, not failure; it is far better to surface the disagreement at day 60 than to discover it at day 270.

The roadmap is not the artifact. The conversation about the roadmap is the artifact. The roadmap exists to make the conversation possible.

Days 60 through 90: visible, agreed-upon execution

The third phase is execution on the small set of items the executive team has agreed to fund and resource at day 60. The set is small on purpose, usually three to five items, because the goal is to deliver visibly against the agreed roadmap, not to demonstrate range.

The right kinds of items for the day-60-to-90 window:

  • A single quick-win infrastructure or identity change with a measurable outcome (Conditional Access tightening, MFA enforcement gap closure, a single high-value Sentinel rule, retiring a known-vulnerable system).
  • A single process commitment that demonstrates governance discipline (the first quarterly board dashboard, the first month of vendor risk reviews on the new cadence, the publication of a sanctioned-AI policy).
  • A single staffing or vendor decision that the inventory phase made obvious and the roadmap memorialized (renegotiating an over-priced contract, decommissioning a duplicate tool, hiring or pausing a hire on the new shape of the team).

The wrong kinds of items: anything that requires more than ninety days to ship, anything that cannot be defended with a one-line business case, and anything that would be controversial inside the executive team. Save the controversial items for after day 90, when you have the credibility to fight for them.

The board introduction

The day-90 board appearance is the introduction, not the program review. The agenda is short: who I am, how I will report (the one-page dashboard, quarterly), the risk picture as the executive team and I have agreed to frame it, and the roadmap with the asks. Twenty minutes of presenting time, twenty minutes of questions, no surprises.

The single most important sentence in the day-90 board appearance is the one that names the cadence. "I will return every quarter with the one-page dashboard, structured against the categories the board already governs, and one written ask if there is one. The program runs on this cadence." Boards trust commitments to cadence more than they trust commitments to outcomes, because cadence is the precondition for governance. The outcomes will come.

The mistakes that wreck the first ninety days

Five patterns recur often enough to be predictable.

Buying a tool in the first month

The vendor pitch will start in week one. The fastest way to lose the executive team's trust is to make a tooling decision before you have the inventory. Tools are easy to buy, hard to retire, and the wrong tool produces operational debt that outlives the original purchase decision by years. Defer every tool conversation until day 60 at the earliest.

Reorganizing the team in the first month

You do not yet know which team members are operating below their potential because of a structural problem and which are operating below their potential because of a tooling problem or a leadership-attention problem. Reorganizing in the first month locks in assumptions you have not yet tested. Wait until you have run a full incident or a full audit response with the team as it stands.

Declaring a quick win prematurely

Wins that are too easy do not move the trust balance. Wins that are too early signal that the new leader is more interested in optics than substance. The right time to declare the first visible win is at the end of the day-90 window, against the roadmap that the executive team has signed off on, with the win framed in the business language the roadmap used.

Bringing the prior engagement's playbook intact

Almost every CISO who has run a security program before has a default sequence of moves. The default sequence was right for the prior organization. It is probably wrong for this one. The discipline is to enter every new engagement with the inventory phase first, even when you can already feel which of your prior moves is going to apply. The ones that apply will still be available at day 60. The ones that do not will save you from the wrong investment.

Reporting to the board before the executive team is aligned

The day-90 board appearance presents an aligned position. If the CEO, CFO, COO, and General Counsel have not all agreed to the framing in advance, do not present it. The executive alignment work must happen in the day-60 leadership session and the two weeks after it. A board appearance that surfaces an internal disagreement for the first time in front of the board is a one-meeting career event.

What hiring managers should be evaluating at week twelve

If you are the board, the CEO, or the executive sponsor of a new healthcare CISO or IT Director, the day-90 review is the highest-leverage evaluation point you have. The questions to be asking:

  • Did the listening tour actually happen? Ask three operational leaders outside the executive team whether the new CISO sat with them, what was discussed, and whether they were heard. The texture of those answers is more telling than any artifact the CISO produces.
  • Is the inventory complete and credible? Pick three random elements (vendor list, BAA file, admin credentials) and check the inventory against them. Gaps at this stage are not disqualifying; opacity about the gaps is.
  • Is the risk register translated into business language? A register that reads like a vulnerability scan output is a sign that the translation work has not been done. A register that reads like the operational language the executive team already uses is the right outcome.
  • Are the asks specific, scoped, and defensible? Vague asks ("more budget for security") are early-warning signs. Specific asks ("an additional security engineer at this seniority by end of Q3, to enable these three roadmap items, with this expected outcome") are what good looks like.
  • Has the new leader committed to a public reporting cadence? A CISO who will not commit to a quarterly reporting cadence at day 90 is unlikely to develop the discipline later. Cadence is the foundation everything else is built on.

The honest answer on metrics

You cannot show meaningful security ROI by day 90. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling. The leading indicators that the program is on track at day 90 are not numerical outcomes; they are structural ones: the inventory exists, the roadmap exists, the executive team has aligned on it, the cadence has been committed to, and the first quarter of execution is visibly underway. The numerical outcomes follow on a six- to twenty-four-month timeline depending on which outcome you are asking about.

This is the part of the role that boards and executive sponsors most often misunderstand. The pressure to show numbers in the first quarter pushes new CISOs toward optics work and away from foundation work. The right counterweight is for the board and CEO to commit, in advance, to evaluating the day-90 deliverables as structural and the day-365 deliverables as numerical. A program built that way ends up producing both. A program built the other way often produces neither.

A note for first-time CISOs

Running this playbook the first time is harder than running it the fourth time, because the discipline to listen and not act runs against the instinct that produced the offer in the first place. The offer was extended because the candidate had a track record of shipping. The job in the first ninety days is not to ship. The discipline of holding that distinction is the actual work of executive transition.

The shortcut, if there is one, is to write the playbook down at the start of the engagement, share it with the CEO and the board sponsor, and treat it as a public commitment. The visibility creates the constraint, and the constraint protects the new leader from their own urge to ship before they understand.
