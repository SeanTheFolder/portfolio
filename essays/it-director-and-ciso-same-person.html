Why the IT Director and the CISO Should Sometimes Be the Same Person

For mid-market healthcare and nonprofit organizations, splitting IT and security across two leaders is often the wrong structural choice. A field case for the unified role at the right organizational scale, and the size at which the split finally makes sense.

For Hiring Managers & Boards ~11 min read Published May 2026 Field essay, leadership practice

There is a default answer in healthcare governance circles that splitting IT and security into two reporting lines is good practice. The argument is one of independence: the security function should be able to escalate against IT operational decisions without a conflict of interest, the CISO should not be reviewing their own controls, the board should hear two voices on technology risk, not one. The argument is sound at the right organizational scale. It is the wrong argument at the scale where most home health, hospice, behavioral health, and small-to-mid-market healthcare nonprofits actually operate. At those scales, the split creates more dysfunction than independence, and the unified role is the better answer until the organization grows past it.

This essay is the case for the unified IT-and-security leadership role, written for the hiring manager who is about to post a job description, the board that is structuring the technology function, and the candidate who is being asked whether they can do both jobs. The honest answer is that at the right scale and with the right person, doing both jobs is structurally better than splitting them. At the wrong scale or with the wrong person, doing both jobs is worse than either alone. The decision is structural, not preferential.

The premise: the split has a real cost that is underweighted

When an organization splits IT and security into two reporting lines, it pays a series of costs that the split's advocates rarely enumerate. Listed plainly:

  • Handoff cost. Every operational decision that has both IT and security implications becomes a coordination event between two teams. Identity changes, infrastructure decisions, vendor onboarding, incident response, change management, all routed through a meeting that the unified role would have made in their own head.
  • Duplicate tooling. The two teams each procure observability, ticketing, asset management, and identity tools that overlap in capability. The licenses cost more than the software does, and the data fragments across two stacks that do not natively integrate.
  • The permanent priority argument. IT operations is measured on uptime, throughput, and user satisfaction. Security is measured on posture, risk reduction, and audit findings. The two metrics produce decisions that conflict at the margin. With one leader, the conflict is a tradeoff. With two leaders, it is a political fight that escalates upward and consumes executive attention.
  • The escalation tax. A separate CISO with a separate reporting line generates more board-level escalations than a unified role. Many of those escalations are about coordination problems the unified role would have absorbed silently.
  • The salary stack. Two senior leaders cost more than one. At an organization of 200-1,000 employees, that delta funds two or three engineering hires the program needs more than it needs the second leader.

None of these costs disappear when the organization is small. They are loudest when the organization is small. The split's advocates tend to dismiss them as transition costs that resolve as the function matures. They do not. They persist for as long as the split persists.

What the unified role actually does

The strongest argument for the unified role is that, at mid-market scale, the work of IT director and the work of CISO are not actually two jobs. They are one job with two regulatory reading frames. Most of the underlying decisions are shared:

Identity is one decision. Whether a user gets MFA, what device they sign in from, what apps they reach, how their access ends — these are operational IT decisions and security control decisions simultaneously. A single leader makes them coherently. Two leaders argue about them.

Vendor selection is one decision. Whether the organization buys a SaaS product is determined by functional fit, integration cost, security posture, contractual terms, and price. The unified role evaluates all five at once. The split arrangement runs the security review as a gate after the IT director has already chosen the vendor, which produces either delayed approvals or perfunctory ones.

Network architecture is one decision. Segmentation, monitoring, remote access, vendor connectivity — these are operational constraints with security implications. The unified role designs them once. The split arrangement designs them twice.

Incident response is one decision. When an incident occurs, the response is operational (restore service) and security (contain, investigate, report) in the same minute. The unified role runs both threads with one set of priorities. The split arrangement runs them in parallel with different incentives, often producing a containment decision that the operations leader undoes for uptime reasons.

At an organization with one EHR, one identity provider, one or two clouds, fewer than five hundred endpoints, and fewer than a hundred SaaS vendors, these decisions are made dozens of times a week. Routing them through two leaders introduces friction that the organization cannot afford without a clear benefit, and the benefit (independence of the security function) is outweighed at this scale by the friction it produces.

1
The Decision-Density Rule

At small-to-mid-market scale, the decisions that need both IT and security input outnumber the decisions that need either alone by a wide margin. The unified role is structurally faster because it has fewer handoffs. The split is structurally slower for exactly the same reason.

The independence argument, examined

The principal argument for splitting is independence. A CISO who reports separately can escalate against the IT operations side without conflict of interest. The CISO can resist a unanimous push to ship a system with known unmitigated risk. The board hears the security voice as distinct from the operational voice. All of these are real concerns.

The structural answer is that independence is a function of how the role is set up, not a function of whether the role is one person or two. A unified IT director and CISO who reports to a board risk committee in addition to the executive team has more independence than a separate CISO who reports through the IT director's chain. A unified role that publishes a quarterly written risk assessment to the board has more independence than a separate CISO who reports verbally to the COO. A unified role with a documented escalation path to outside counsel and the board chair has more independence than a separate CISO whose reporting line stops at the CFO.

The independence the split is meant to produce can be produced by reporting lines, written commitments, and external accountability. None of those require two people. They require designing the role.

There is one exception: when the organization's culture has a history of overriding security decisions for operational expedience. In a culture where the leadership team will pressure a unified role to under-call risks, splitting the function is a way to put resistance on the org chart. That is a real signal that splitting is justified. It is also a signal that the underlying culture problem will continue to produce dysfunction regardless of structure, and the split is treating a symptom.

What the unified role requires of the person

The unified role is not the same as the split done by one person. It requires a leader with a particular shape:

Hands-on technical depth. The leader has to be able to read the configurations they are governing. A unified leader who delegates the entire technical layer becomes a meeting attender; the role's leverage comes from the leader's ability to reason directly about the systems. This is the single largest hiring filter and the one most often missed. A leader who stopped being technical five years ago is not a candidate for the unified role.

Translation between technical and business language. The unified leader speaks to caregivers, the executive team, the board, the regulator, and the helpdesk. Each of those audiences requires a different translation, and the leader has to do the translation in real time. Candidates from pure-security backgrounds often struggle with the operations and clinical sides; candidates from pure-IT backgrounds often struggle with the regulatory and risk sides. The unified leader has to be comfortable across all of them.

Calibrated risk thinking. The leader will be making operational decisions with risk implications dozens of times a week. Heat-map thinking is not adequate. The leader needs to be able to estimate probability and impact in dollars, defend the estimates, and adjust them as evidence comes in. This skill is rarer than it sounds and very visible in interviews if the interviewer asks for an estimate of a real risk.

The patience to do small things well. The unified role's daily work is unglamorous. Patching cadence, ticket queues, vendor renewals, account reviews. A leader who is bored by these is a leader who will let them slip. The candidate's last two roles are diagnostic: did they personally run an MFA rollout, an EHR integration, a vendor decommission, an audit response? The candidate who has done these recently can do them again. The candidate who has not is a posture risk.

A unified role staffed by the wrong person is worse than the split. The cost of getting it wrong is higher because the consequences are concentrated in one seat rather than two.

The economics of the unified role at mid-market scale

An organization with 200-1,500 employees, one to a few clinical systems, modest cloud footprint, and HIPAA-grade regulatory exposure is the typical mid-market healthcare scale. The unified role's salary at that scale is competitive with a senior IT director or a junior CISO individually. The split's salary stack is roughly 1.6x the unified salary at the same scale.

The delta is meaningful. At a 500-person organization, the split's premium funds a senior security engineer or a senior systems administrator. Both of those are higher-leverage hires for a program at that scale than a second senior leader. The unified role plus a senior engineer is structurally stronger than the split with no engineering bench, and the budget supports the first arrangement and not the second.

This is the math the board should be doing. A second leader is not free. The cost of the second leader is the engineering hire that did not happen, the tool license that was not bought, the audit that was not commissioned. At small-to-mid-market scale, those tradeoffs are real and visible.

A second senior leader is not free. The cost of the second leader is the engineering hire that did not happen, the tool license that was not bought, the audit that was not commissioned.

The size at which the split makes sense

The split becomes structurally correct as the organization grows past the scale where a single leader can hold the operational picture in their head and the security posture in their head simultaneously. Practical signals that the split is now appropriate:

  • The organization has more than three EHR or major clinical systems with separate operational ownership.
  • The endpoint estate exceeds approximately 1,500-2,000 devices under management.
  • The vendor footprint exceeds approximately 200 active third-party relationships with PHI access.
  • The organization is under continuous regulatory scrutiny (HHS resolution agreement, multi-state DOI oversight, an active assessment program from a payer or accrediting body).
  • The security function has a team of more than three or four people that benefits from a dedicated senior leader.
  • The board has formally established a separate audit and risk committee that meets independently from the rest of governance.

If three or more of those signals are present, the unified role is past its operating range, and the split becomes the right structural answer. If fewer than three are present, the split's costs are likely outweighing its benefits.

2
The Three-Signal Rule

The split makes sense when at least three of the structural signals are present: clinical-system count, endpoint scale, vendor scale, regulatory scrutiny, security team size, board governance maturity. Below that threshold, the unified role is structurally cleaner. Above it, the split is structurally cleaner. The decision is read off the org, not off the org-chart fashion.

What hiring managers should screen for

Hiring managers writing a job description for the unified role should screen for a different shape of candidate than they would for either standalone role. The unified candidate is rarer than the standalone candidate; the search takes longer; the bar is higher. What to look for:

  • Recent hands-on work on both sides. Not "managed a team that did" but "personally configured, deployed, or remediated."
  • Evidence of running a board-facing risk conversation. Not just attended one — produced the artifact and answered the questions.
  • A track record of programs that shipped, with calibrated outcomes. Numbers, not adjectives.
  • A working relationship with regulators or auditors. The unified role spends more of its time there than either standalone role does.
  • The ability to write. Most of the unified role's leverage comes through written artifacts — the risk assessment, the board memo, the policy update, the incident report. A candidate who cannot write clearly cannot do the job.

What to avoid: candidates whose recent work has been entirely strategic with no operational depth, candidates whose security experience is all framework work and no implementation, candidates whose IT experience is all infrastructure and no risk thinking, and candidates whose answer to "tell me about a recent risk decision you made in dollars" is a heat-map color.

What the candidate should ask

Candidates evaluating a unified role should ask about the structure as carefully as the hiring manager evaluates them. The structural questions:

  • Who does the role report to? Is there a dotted line to the board risk committee or audit committee?
  • Is there a documented escalation path that does not pass through the role's primary report?
  • What is the engineering bench? A unified role with no senior engineers under it is a leader who will be doing all of the work themselves.
  • How does the organization currently make security tradeoff decisions, and where will the candidate's authority actually land in those decisions?
  • What is the budget the role controls, and what budget does it have to advocate for elsewhere?
  • What is the recent posture? Audits passed, incidents survived, programs shipped — these are diagnostic of whether the role's predecessor had the authority and resources to do the job.

A unified role at an organization that wants the title without the structural commitment is a role that will burn out the candidate. A unified role at an organization that has thought through the structural commitments is one of the most rewarding shapes of leadership work in healthcare, because the leader can actually move the program in the directions they think are right, without the political tax of a parallel reporting line.
