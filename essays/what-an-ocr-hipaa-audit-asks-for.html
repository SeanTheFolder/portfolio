What an OCR HIPAA Audit Actually Asks For

An OCR audit is not a security test. It is a document request with a ten-business-day clock attached. The single most-cited finding across the entire enforcement history is an inadequate or absent risk analysis. The organizations that come through it well are not the ones with the best security; they are the ones whose evidence binder already exists. This is the field guide to what the request contains and how to be ready before the notice arrives.

For Compliance Officers, CISOs & Boards ~13 min read Published May 2026 Field essay, audit readiness practice

The email arrives on a Tuesday. It is from the Department of Health and Human Services, Office for Civil Rights, and it is polite, specific, and short. It notifies the organization that it has been selected for a HIPAA audit, names a point of contact, and attaches a document request list. Somewhere in the cover language is a deadline: the requested documents are due within ten business days. For an organization that has treated HIPAA compliance as an annual checkbox, that Tuesday is the beginning of a three-week scramble that will consume the compliance officer, the IT director, and eventually the CEO, and will probably still produce an incomplete response. For an organization that has treated the regulatory stack as a design input, that Tuesday is a filing exercise: the binder already exists, the documents are current, and the response goes out in four days with time to spare. The difference between those two organizations is not the quality of their security. It is whether the evidence was assembled before the notice or after it.

This essay is a working guide to what an OCR audit actually requests, why the risk analysis sits at the center of all of it, and how to build the evidence binder so that an audit notice is a confirmation event rather than an emergency. It is written for the compliance officer who owns the response, the CISO or IT director who has to produce the technical evidence, and the board member who wants to understand what exposure the organization is actually carrying. It is grounded in the OCR audit protocol, the published enforcement record, and the direction the Security Rule is heading.

What an OCR audit is, and what it is not

Precision matters here, because organizations conflate three different things and prepare for the wrong one. An OCR audit is a proactive compliance review: OCR selects covered entities and business associates, requests documentation, and assesses whether the organization's HIPAA program meets the requirements of the Privacy, Security, and Breach Notification Rules. It is not triggered by a complaint or a breach. It is a planned program activity, and the current audit phase is focused on the Security Rule, with the risk analysis requirement as the explicit centerpiece.

That is distinct from an OCR investigation, which is triggered by a filed complaint or a reported breach and is adversarial in posture from the first contact. It is also distinct from a CMS survey, which assesses Conditions of Participation for clinical operations, and from an OIG review, which targets fraud, waste, and abuse in billing. An organization can face all four, and each wants different evidence. The audit is the most predictable of them, because OCR publishes the audit protocol — the actual list of what auditors evaluate and the established performance criteria for each item. The protocol is the open-book portion of the exam, and most organizations never read it until the notice arrives.

The practical consequence is that an audit is winnable in advance in a way an investigation is not. The criteria are published. The document categories are knowable. The only variable the organization controls is whether the evidence exists and is current on the day the request lands. Everything in this essay is downstream of that single fact.

The risk analysis is the center of gravity

If there is one thing to understand about OCR enforcement, it is this: the inadequate or absent risk analysis is the most frequently cited deficiency in the entire history of HIPAA enforcement. It appears in resolution agreement after resolution agreement, across organizations of every size, from solo practices to national health systems. When OCR levies a seven-figure settlement, the risk analysis failing is almost always in the findings. It is the load-bearing requirement of the Security Rule, and it is the one organizations most reliably get wrong.

The requirement lives at 45 CFR §164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A), and the regulatory language is exact: the organization must “conduct an accurate and thorough assessment of the potential risks and vulnerabilities to the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of electronic protected health information held by the” organization. Two words in that sentence do most of the work. Accurate means it has to reflect the organization's actual environment, not a template. Thorough means it has to cover all ePHI everywhere it lives, not just the EHR. An assessment that misses the laptops, the backup vendor, the imaging system, the email archive, the texting app the nurses actually use, or the analytics warehouse is, by definition, neither accurate nor thorough, and OCR has cited exactly those omissions.

The companion requirement is risk management, at §164.308(a)(1)(ii)(B): having identified the risks, the organization must implement security measures sufficient to reduce them to a reasonable and appropriate level. This is the pairing that organizations break most often. They commission a risk analysis, receive a report, file it, and never execute the remediation. OCR reads that as two findings, not one: the analysis may have been adequate, but the failure to act on it is its own violation. A risk analysis with no corresponding risk management plan and no evidence of remediation progress is, in OCR's eyes, close to having done nothing.

1
The Risk-Analysis-Is-Not-a-Gap-Analysis Rule

OCR has stated directly, in published guidance and in resolution agreements, that a gap analysis is not a risk analysis. A gap analysis checks controls against a framework checklist. A risk analysis identifies where ePHI lives, enumerates the threats and vulnerabilities to it, assesses the likelihood and impact of each, and assigns a risk level. The two are different documents with different methods. Substituting the easier one for the required one is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes in HIPAA compliance.

This distinction deserves dwelling on because it is the trap that catches well-intentioned organizations. A gap analysis is a useful and legitimate exercise: it maps the current control set against a standard like the CIS Controls or the HIPAA Security Rule implementation specifications and identifies which controls are missing. But it answers the question “which controls do we lack?” not the question “what are the risks to our ePHI and how bad are they?” The risk analysis has to start from the data, not the controls. It has to inventory every system and flow that touches ePHI, name the realistic threats to each, evaluate the existing safeguards, and produce a rated list of risks. OCR has explicitly rejected gap analyses submitted in place of risk analyses, and the organizations that learned this did so the expensive way.

What the document request actually contains

The specific request list varies by audit, but the categories are consistent and they map directly to the published protocol. An organization that has these categories assembled and current is most of the way to a clean response. The recurring categories are:

The current risk analysis. Dated within the audit period, covering all ePHI, with a documented methodology. OCR wants to see how the organization identified its ePHI, what threats and vulnerabilities it considered, and how it arrived at its risk ratings. A two-page summary will not satisfy this. A defensible risk analysis for a mid-sized organization runs to dozens of pages because the environment does.

The risk management plan. The remediation roadmap that flows from the risk analysis, with risks prioritized, owners assigned, target dates set, and evidence of progress against prior items. This is what demonstrates the §164.308(a)(1)(ii)(B) requirement. OCR will compare the risks named in the analysis against the items in the management plan and look for the ones that fell through.

The Security Rule policies and procedures. Written documentation for the administrative, physical, and technical safeguards. Not a generic template with the organization's name pasted in, but policies that describe what the organization actually does. OCR auditors are practiced at spotting purchased templates that bear no relationship to the organization's real operations.

Evidence of implementation. This is the category organizations most underestimate. A policy is a claim. OCR wants the proof that the claim is true: the access-review logs, the audit-log configurations, the workforce training completion records, the sanction policy enforcement records, the encryption settings, the contingency plan test results, the workstation security standards as actually deployed. A policy that says “we review access quarterly” with no review records behind it is worse than no policy, because it documents a commitment the organization failed to keep.

The breach and incident log. Documentation of security incidents and breaches, the risk assessments performed for each, the notification decisions, and the corrective actions. Even an organization with no reportable breaches needs the log that shows it was tracking and assessing incidents.

Business associate agreements and the BAA inventory. The signed agreements and, increasingly, evidence that the organization actually manages the relationships behind them. A signed BAA in a folder is the floor, not the control, and OCR's interest in vendor oversight is rising.

Workforce training records. Evidence that the workforce received security awareness training, when, and on what content. Completion rates that show the training actually happened, not just that it was assigned.

2
The Evidence-of-Implementation Rule

For every policy the organization can produce, OCR will ask for the evidence that the policy is operating. A policy without operational proof is not a partial credit; it is an affirmative liability, because it documents an intention the organization did not fulfill. Build the program so that every written safeguard generates an artifact — a log, a report, a record, a screenshot — that proves it runs. The artifact is the evidence. The policy is just the claim.

The ten-business-day clock is the whole game

The reason audit readiness is a binary — either you are ready or you are not — is the response window. OCR audits give organizations a short, fixed period to produce the requested documents, historically on the order of ten business days. Ten business days is two calendar weeks. It is not enough time to conduct a risk analysis. It is not enough time to write a policy set from scratch. It is not enough time to retroactively generate a year of access-review records that were never kept. It is barely enough time to find and organize documents that already exist, which is the entire point.

This is why the work has to be done before the notice. The risk analysis takes weeks to do properly. The evidence of implementation accumulates over months of actually running the program. The policy set reflects decisions made and documented over time. None of it can be manufactured inside the response window, and OCR knows this. The audit is, in a real sense, testing whether the program existed before the auditor showed up, and the response window is calibrated specifically to make after-the-fact fabrication impossible. An organization that tries to build its evidence inside the window produces a response that looks exactly like what it is.

3
The Binder-Before-the-Notice Rule

Maintain a standing audit binder — physical, digital, or both — that holds the current risk analysis, the risk management plan, the policy set, and a living index of where the implementation evidence lives. Refresh it on a calendar, not on a trigger. When the notice arrives, the response is assembly and review, not creation. The organizations that survive OCR audits well are the ones for whom the audit changes their week, not their quarter.

The bar is rising: the Security Rule overhaul

The readiness standard is not static, and the direction of travel is toward more prescription, not less. The proposed overhaul of the HIPAA Security Rule, published in early 2025 and moving through the rulemaking process, signals where the requirements are heading, and the direction is unambiguous: the era of the “addressable” safeguard as an optional one is ending. The proposal would remove the longstanding distinction between “required” and “addressable” implementation specifications, making nearly all of them mandatory with only narrow, documented exceptions. It would explicitly require a technology asset inventory and a network map — the very things a thorough risk analysis already depends on. It would require multi-factor authentication, encryption of ePHI at rest and in transit, vulnerability scanning, penetration testing, and regular compliance audits, all as baseline obligations rather than risk-based options.

Whatever the final form and timeline, the planning posture is clear. An organization building toward the proposed standard today is building toward where enforcement is going regardless of the exact effective date, and an organization that builds a CIS Controls v8 program with a HIPAA crosswalk is, not coincidentally, building most of what the proposed rule will require. The asset inventory, the MFA enforcement, the encryption, the audit logging, the vulnerability management — these are the same controls under different names. The organizations that treated the “addressable” specifications as genuinely optional are the ones with the most ground to cover, and the audit program is the mechanism that will surface the gap.

An OCR audit notice does not ask whether the organization is secure. It asks whether the organization can prove, on ten business days' notice, that it understood its risks and acted on them. Most organizations cannot, and the reason is never the security. It is the evidence.

What does not work: failure modes from the field

Five patterns recur in organizations that respond poorly to OCR audits, and each is preventable.

Failure mode one: the stale risk analysis. The organization commissioned a risk analysis three years ago, filed the report, and never updated it. The environment has changed — new systems, new vendors, a cloud migration, an acquisition — and the analysis no longer reflects reality. OCR reads a stale analysis as no analysis, because it fails the “accurate” test. The risk analysis is a living document on an annual cadence, not a one-time deliverable.

Failure mode two: the gap analysis in the risk analysis's chair. The organization paid a vendor for a “HIPAA assessment” that turned out to be a controls checklist, submits it as its risk analysis, and discovers in the findings that OCR does not accept it. The fix is to know the difference before the notice and to ensure the document on file is an actual risk analysis with a data inventory, threat enumeration, and risk ratings.

Failure mode three: policies with no evidence behind them. The organization has a complete, well-written policy set and almost no proof that any of it operates. The access reviews are not documented, the training records are incomplete, the contingency plan was never tested. The polished policy binder makes this worse, because it raises the expectation that the evidence exists and then fails to deliver it. Every policy needs its operational artifact.

Failure mode four: the risk analysis with no management plan. The organization did a genuine, thorough risk analysis, identified real risks, and then did nothing with it. The remediation never got funded, the items never got owners, the high risks are still open a year later. OCR cites the failure to manage identified risks as its own violation, distinct from the analysis. The analysis without the plan is a documented confession that the organization knew its risks and chose not to address them.

Failure mode five: the scramble response. The organization receives the notice and attempts to build everything inside the ten-day window — conducting a hurried risk analysis, back-dating records, assembling a policy set overnight. The response is visibly assembled under duress, the inconsistencies are obvious, and the attempt to fabricate evidence inside the window can convert a manageable finding into an aggravating factor. The only winning move is to have done the work before the notice.

What a credible response looks like

The organization that responds well to an OCR audit does a recognizable set of things, and none of them happen inside the response window. It maintains a current risk analysis, refreshed annually and after any material environmental change, that inventories all ePHI and rates the risks to it. It runs a risk management plan that turns those ratings into owned, dated, funded remediation items, with evidence of progress. It keeps a policy set that describes what it actually does, and behind every policy, an artifact that proves the policy operates. It logs its incidents and breaches and the assessments behind its notification decisions. It manages its business associate relationships beyond the signed agreement. And it keeps all of this in a standing binder with a living index, refreshed on a calendar, so that the response to a notice is a review-and-assemble exercise measured in days.

That organization treats the board as part of the readiness posture. The one-page risk dashboard that reports security to the board in business terms is also, not coincidentally, the executive summary of the audit posture: it shows that the organization understands its top risks, is tracking them over time, and is funding their remediation. When the audit notice arrives, the board has already been seeing the substance of the response every quarter. The audit confirms what the dashboard has been saying, rather than contradicting it.

None of this is exotic. It is the ordinary output of a security program that was built to operate rather than to pass an inspection. The organizations that struggle with OCR audits are, almost without exception, the ones that built for the inspection — the policy binder, the annual checkbox, the filed report — rather than for the operation. The audit is designed to tell the difference, and it reliably does.

The OCR audit readiness binder structure, the risk-analysis methodology template that satisfies the “accurate and thorough” standard, the risk-analysis-versus-gap-analysis comparison, the document-request response checklist, and the evidence-of-implementation mapping that ties each policy to its proving artifact are available on request to peers, compliance officers, and board members. Mention the organization's size band and current risk-analysis state on the request; the materials calibrate accordingly.
