Philosophy

Security is not a cost center. It’s a trust engine.

Years at the intersection of healthcare, compliance, and technology have shaped a clear set of principles. These guide every decision, from how I present risk to a board to how I configure a firewall rule.

01 / Core beliefs

Nine principles

No. 1

Security enables the mission

In healthcare, security makes “yes” possible safely. Every control protects the ability to serve patients.

No. 2

Translate, don’t overwhelm

The most dangerous CISO is the one who can’t explain risk in a board meeting. Converting CVEs into funded decisions is core.

No. 3

Compliance is a floor

Passing an audit proves a minimum on one day. Real security is adaptive and built into culture.

No. 4

Constraints drive creativity

Non-profit budgets demand ruthless prioritization. Enterprise-grade posture comes from smarter decisions, not bigger spend.

No. 5

Own the whole stack

Security lives in every contract, config, onboarding flow, and board presentation, not just the tools labeled “security.”

No. 6

AI governance is data governance

Build the boundary in Purview and Conditional Access first. The agent is the interface; the boundary is the governance.

No. 7

Translation is the job

Security leadership converts one risk reality into five vocabularies. Build artifacts that bound the translation. That is what keeps the role survivable.

No. 8

Models are rented, governance is owned

Single-vendor LLM lock-in is a strategic mistake in 2026. The asset is the governance trunk every model passes through.

No. 9

Org structure is a security control

The reporting line, the unified-or-split role decision, the fractional-plus-FTE pairing. These are control decisions, not org-chart preferences.

02 / How I operate

The leadership framework

  1. Assess first

    Start with the actual risk landscape, not inherited assumptions.

  2. Prioritize ruthlessly

    Not every vulnerability is critical. Knowing the difference is the job.

  3. Communicate up

    Technical findings become decisions when framed in business terms.

  4. Execute with precision

    Strategy without implementation is noise.

03 / In depth

Positions, argued

Why Microsoft is the primary ecosystem

For a lean team in a regulated environment, the integration between Sentinel, Defender, Purview, Intune, and Entra ID multiplies capability: one identity plane, one audit trail, one policy engine. Best-of-breed still wins where the gap justifies the complexity: WatchGuard at the network edge, Ubiquiti and Aruba in the fabric, Tenable for vulnerability management. The default, though, is the stack that makes one engineer operate like five.

AI in healthcare security: practical, not theoretical

Copilot Studio agents run in production under the same governance rigor as any system: Purview DLP on prompt inputs, Conditional Access on agent surfaces, audit logging through Sentinel. Security Copilot triages alerts and drafts KQL. The governance pattern is reusable; the boundary is what the organization owns; the model is what it rents.

Multi-LLM is a portfolio decision, not a vendor decision

The capability differential between Claude, Gemini, GPT, and Copilot is large enough that per-task selection produces materially better outcomes. The operational cost of carrying two or three approved models is small if the governance trunk is built right, and the trunk is the strategic asset. The full thesis is in the multi-LLM essay.

The unified IT-and-CISO role at the right scale

For organizations of roughly 50 to 400 staff, splitting IT and security across two leaders is often the wrong structural choice. The split creates handoff cost, duplicated tooling, and a permanent priority argument. The unified role runs faster, hires fewer people more thoughtfully, and holds a tighter audit posture. The right time to split is when each role justifies a full week of strategic work plus a team underneath it, which is a higher threshold than most assume. The unified-role essay covers the trigger points.

Org design is the quietest security control

The most consequential security decisions are often structural: who reports to whom, whether the security lead has authority over IT, whether the first hire is an engineer or a leader, and who carries the translation load. These determine whether the program ships, whether people stay, and whether the audit posture is real or theater. Burnout is structural, not workload. The retention essay goes deeper.

Digital transformation is security work

The HubSpot CMS build, the Webflow rebuild, the ADP implementation: none of these were diversions from security. Paper-based processes are a data governance risk. Unmaintained sites are attack surface. Done right, digital transformation reduces attack surface and improves auditability at the same time.

Physical security is part of the security program

Physical and digital security are the same discipline with different attack surfaces. Tailgating is credential theft. The DMP Entré access control and Ubiquiti camera systems are treated identically to any other system: documented, enrolled, governed, and audited. Door events route to the same audit trail as logical access.

The healthcare regulatory stack is a design input

HIPAA Security and Privacy Rules, HITECH breach notification, 42 CFR Part 418, OIG compliance program guidance for home health and hospice, the Information Blocking Rule, 42 CFR Part 2 in active enforcement since February 2026, OCR’s third audit phase, and the False Claims Act sitting behind every billing system. Compliance is an architectural principle, not an annual project. Treat the regulatory stack as a design input and the audit becomes a confirmation event.

04 / Executive conversations

The questions boards and hiring panels actually ask

You rose from IT Specialist to CISO in three years at the same organization. How do we know that’s merit and not title inflation?

The evidence is in the implementations. There was no SIEM, no MDM, and no documented security posture when I arrived. Everything in the project portfolio was built by hand while running IT operations. Each promotion followed a demonstrable expansion of scope. The title caught up to the work, not the other way around.

What does the healthcare compliance landscape look like for a home health and hospice CISO?

Layered and actively enforced: HIPAA Security and Privacy Rules as the baseline, HITECH for breach notification, OIG compliance program guidance with separate fraud-and-abuse risk areas for home health and for hospice, 42 CFR Part 418 for clinical documentation integrity, 42 CFR Part 2 for SUD records in active enforcement since February 2026, the Information Blocking Rule for data access, and the False Claims Act behind every billing system. HITRUST CSF ties it into a single program. Missing any piece means operating blind.

How do you present security risk to a board without a technical background?

Never lead with CVEs or patch counts. Every significant risk translates to one of three business terms: operational continuity, regulatory exposure, reputational cost. A critical vulnerability in a patient-facing system is not “CVSS 9.8.” It is breach notification, six-figure remediation, and an OCR investigation. I maintain a standing one-page risk dashboard: top risks mapped to business impact, quarterly trend, never more than one page. The board makes decisions; it does not read reports.

Your career has been largely in one sector and one organization. Is that a limitation?

Reframe it: I built a mature security program from nothing, in a regulated environment, on a nonprofit budget, without a breach. That is harder than inheriting a program. The consulting years covered commercial, hospitality, retail, and creative sectors across infrastructure, physical security, and development. Depth at Harbors proves sustained execution; the early years prove adaptability.

What’s your approach to AI governance in a HIPAA-regulated environment?

The CISO leads the AI conversation rather than reacting to it. Copilot Studio agents ship with the same governance rigor as any system: Purview DLP on prompt inputs, Conditional Access on agent surfaces, Sentinel audit logging, and a defined data boundary per agent. Shadow AI is a DLP and data-governance problem, not a policy memo: block what is not approved, fast-track approval for legitimate business use.

How do you balance being technically hands-on with operating at the executive level?

In a lean organization you don’t get to choose. I write KQL, configure WatchGuard policy, and have physically run cable. That context prevents vendors from selling vaporware and prevents real risk from being buried in jargon. I deliberately own architecture decisions, detection logic, and HIPAA data flows, and step back from repeatable operational work that can be documented and delegated. The line is high-judgment versus repeatable, not strategic versus technical.

What does your first 90 days look like in a new organization?

Days 1 to 30: listen and assess. The real problem, the constraints, the regulatory exposure as it exists in reality rather than on the org chart. No changes. Days 30–60: a written risk assessment and a prioritized roadmap in business-impact terms, presented and aligned before anything gets built. Days 60–90: execute the highest-priority items with visible, measurable outcomes. By day 90 there is at least one tangible security improvement and leadership has a clear picture of where we’re heading and why.

Full-time, consulting, or both?

Both, depending on fit. Full-time for organizations where security is a genuine strategic priority rather than a compliance checkbox. Consulting for organizations that need a program built or matured, a specific compliance challenge navigated, or experienced hands on a high-stakes implementation. I don’t do security theater on either side.

Should the IT director and the CISO be the same person?

At mid-market healthcare and nonprofit scale, roughly 50 to 400 staff, yes, more often than not. The split creates handoff cost, duplicated tooling, a permanent change-window argument, and two budget asks for one stack. The right time to split is when each role justifies a full week of strategic work and a team underneath it. That threshold is higher than most assume.

How do you think about multi-LLM strategy versus a single-vendor commitment?

A Copilot commitment is a reason to use Copilot where it is best, not a reason to default to it everywhere. The capability differential between Claude, Gemini, GPT, and Copilot in 2026 is large enough that per-task selection outperforms single-model defaults, and the incremental cost of carrying two or three models is small for a 200-user organization if the governance is built right. The asset is the trunk every model passes through: Purview labels, Conditional Access, Sentinel logging. The model is the rented part; the trunk is owned.
